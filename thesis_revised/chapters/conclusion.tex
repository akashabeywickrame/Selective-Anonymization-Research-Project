\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

I set out to blur bystanders in first-person video while keeping the people the wearer is
with visible, using only the glasses' camera and without recognising anyone. This chapter
answers the three research questions from Section~\ref{sec:rq}, summarises the
contributions, and sets out what I would do next.

\subsection{Answers to the Research Questions}

\paragraph{RQ1: Can simple visual cues separate partners from bystanders without
identity recognition?}
Yes, with a clear bias towards privacy. Face size and position alone, the Phase~2a cues,
already separated the two groups in most cases. Adding head pose and mouth movement made
the system more conservative: in the video-clip study it left only one of 33 bystanders
visible, and 39 of the 40 faces it kept visible were genuine partners. The cues do not
recognise everyone the wearer is with, however. Partners whose faces appear small, such
as friends across a table, fall below the proximity threshold, and the system often
confirms partners only after a delay. Neither failure requires identity information to fix.

\paragraph{RQ2: How well does selective anonymization protect bystanders and preserve
partners compared with blurring every face?}
Blanket blurring protects every bystander but keeps no partner visible. The live system
protected 96.97\% of bystanders while keeping 56.52\% of partners visible. For recorded
video, the offline second pass raised partner preservation to 78.26\% while still
protecting 93.94\% of bystanders. Selective anonymization therefore gives up a few
percentage points of bystander protection to recover most of what blanket blurring
destroys. These figures come from five annotators with almost perfect agreement, but from
a dataset of 21 videos, so they should be confirmed on more varied footage.

\paragraph{RQ3: How much computation does it need, and is it feasible on wearable-class
hardware?}
The classification itself costs almost nothing: 0.02~ms per frame. The cost lies in face
detection and, for Phase~2b, in landmark extraction. On a laptop CPU the pipeline runs at
9.4 to 11.6 frames per second, which is practical for recorded video but not for live
display. On a Raspberry Pi~5 the current implementation would reach only about 1.5 frames
per second. Projections from published benchmarks show that converting the detector to a
faster format alone would roughly triple this, and that the neural processing units
already present in camera-glasses chips could remove most of the detection cost. Live
operation on glasses is therefore feasible in principle, but it needs that engineering work
and has not been demonstrated here.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item A pipeline that separates interaction partners from bystanders using only face
        geometry, head pose and mouth movement from a single RGB camera, with no face
        recognition and no stored identity.
  \item A two-rule offline pass for recorded video (a retroactive latch and a group rule)
        that keeps the people the wearer is with visible for their whole time on screen.
  \item An evaluation method for this task: blind labelling of short video clips with
        sound by several annotators, agreement measured with Cohen's and Fleiss' kappa,
        bystander protection and partner preservation reported separately with confidence
        intervals, and the system's decisions checked against its rendered output.
  \item A breakdown of where the processing time goes, with projections to a
        Raspberry Pi~5 and to current camera-glasses hardware.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Work}

\paragraph{Fix the distance problem.}
Most missed partners were friends who appear small in the frame. A first step is to
relax the area threshold when the landmark cues confirm a face that is turned towards the
wearer and speaking. The landmark cues also need to work on small faces, which could be
tested by enlarging the face crop before passing it to MediaPipe.

\paragraph{Test the second pass on new data.}
The retroactive latch and the group rule were designed after looking at the video-clip
errors. I would label a fresh set of clips from different videos, with annotators who do
not know the system, and measure both rules on it.

\paragraph{Bring the group rule to live use.}
Both second-pass rules need to see the future of the recording. Delaying the live output
by a few seconds would give the system time to confirm partners and group members before
their frames are shown. The useful experiment is to measure how much partner preservation
each extra second of delay buys.

\paragraph{Measure on real hardware.}
I would run the pipeline on a Raspberry Pi~5 with the detector exported to NCNN, and
measure both frame rate and power draw, then test running the detector only on every
second or third frame and measure what that costs in accuracy.

\paragraph{Broaden the dataset.}
The dataset lacks outdoor conversations and indoor crowds in which the wearer talks to no
one. Recording such scenes with consenting participants would also allow audio to be used
alongside the mouth cue, which would distinguish speaking from laughing or eating.
