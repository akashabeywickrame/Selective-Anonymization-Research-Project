\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Research Design}
\label{sec:design}

I followed a build-and-evaluate design in four stages (Figure~\ref{fig:research_design}).
I first assembled a dataset of first-person video, then built a five-stage pipeline that
detects, tracks, classifies and selectively blurs faces, and ran it over every video while
logging each decision. Finally I evaluated the decisions against human judgements and
against a blanket-blurring baseline, and measured the processing time. The rest of the
chapter follows the same order: the materials (Section~\ref{sec:materials}), the theory
behind each stage (Section~\ref{sec:theory}), the pipeline
(Sections~\ref{sec:arch} to~\ref{sec:secondpass}) and the evaluation
(Section~\ref{sec:evaldesign}).

\subsection{Materials}
\label{sec:materials}

\subsubsection{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

\paragraph{Source and selection.}
No public dataset labels which people in first-person video are interacting with the
camera wearer and which are bystanders, so I assembled my own from two sources, one for
each kind of scene. I did not record any footage myself. A video was included if it was
filmed from a first-person viewpoint (a camera worn on the head or held at eye level),
contained faces large enough to be detected, and clearly belonged to one of two
categories:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Interaction} (14 videos), taken from the first-person video released with
        Meta's EgoBlur work \cite{egoblur2023}. In these videos the wearer spends time with
        other people, mostly playing card or board games around a table, and in some videos
        shopping in small stores and talking to staff. They contain both partners and
        bystanders, for example other customers in a shop. Each is a five-minute segment.
  \item \textbf{Bystander} (7 videos), cut from first-person walking videos published on
        YouTube. The wearer walks through busy outdoor places such as street markets and
        city streets without talking to anyone, so every face is a bystander. I deliberately
        took the clips from different cities and countries, by day and at night, so that
        the bystanders cover a wide range of places, crowd sizes, lighting and appearance
        rather than one location. Each clip was cut to the part with the most passers-by,
        giving lengths of 6 to 78 seconds.
\end{itemize} Source resolutions ranged from
$1280 \times 720$ to $3840 \times 2160$, and I resized every frame to $1280 \times 720$
before processing so that all thresholds apply to the same frame size.

\paragraph{Size of the dataset.}
Counting only videos would understate how much data the system processes, because each
video contains many people and each person appears in many frames.
Table~\ref{tab:dataset} gives the dataset at three levels: videos, frames, and face
tracks. A face track is one person followed through consecutive frames by the tracker,
and it is the unit the classifier makes decisions about.

\paragraph{Balance.}
By duration the dataset is dominated by interaction video (70 of 73 minutes). The
imbalance between partners and bystanders is smaller than this suggests, because the
interaction videos also contain many bystanders: other customers, people at other tables
and passers-by. I handled the remaining imbalance in three ways. The labelled evaluation
samples were drawn from every video, so short bystander videos are represented alongside
long interaction videos. I report bystander protection and partner preservation as two
separate rates (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}), so a large class cannot hide poor
performance on a small one, as it would with overall accuracy. And I report results
separately for the two video categories.

\paragraph{Ground truth.}
Whether a person is interacting with the wearer is a human judgement, so the ground truth
comes from human annotators. I built three labelled sets over the course of the project,
each improving on the previous one (Section~\ref{sec:groundtruth}). Their reliability is
checked by measuring agreement between independent annotators (Section~\ref{sec:stats}).

\paragraph{Handling of personal data.}
The videos show members of the public. I kept the videos and all frames derived from them
on my own computer, shared clips only with the annotators for labelling, and did not
publish any frame that shows a face. No figure in this thesis shows a person's face.

\subsubsection{Hardware and software}

All processing was done on a laptop with an Intel Core i5-13420H processor and no use of
its graphics processor. I implemented the pipeline in Python 3.13 with OpenCV 4.13 for
video input and image processing, Ultralytics 8.4 for face detection \cite{yolov8}, my own
implementation of SORT with the FilterPy Kalman filter and SciPy's assignment solver for
tracking, and the MediaPipe 0.10 Face Landmarker \cite{lugaresi2019mediapipe} for facial
landmarks. Every parameter of the pipeline is held in one configuration file, so each
experiment in Chapter~\ref{sec:results} differs from the main run only in the parameters
it names.

\subsection{Theoretical Background}
\label{sec:theory}

This section gives the theory behind each pipeline stage. Sections~\ref{sec:detection}
to~\ref{sec:anon} then describe how I applied it.

\subsubsection{Bounding-box overlap and non-maximum suppression}

The overlap between two boxes $A$ and $B$ is measured by their intersection over union,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{IoU}(A, B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|},
  \label{eq:iou}
\end{equation}
which is 1 for identical boxes and 0 for boxes that do not touch. A detector usually
predicts several overlapping boxes for the same face. Non-maximum suppression (NMS) keeps
the box with the highest confidence, removes every other box whose IoU with it exceeds a
threshold $\tau_{\mathrm{NMS}}$, and repeats with the remaining boxes. IoU is used again
by the tracker to decide which detection belongs to which track.

\subsubsection{The Kalman filter}

The tracker predicts where each face will be in the next frame with a Kalman filter, which
estimates the state of a moving object from noisy measurements. In my implementation the
state of a face is its four box corners and their velocities,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x} = [x_1,\, y_1,\, x_2,\, y_2,\, \dot{x}_1,\, \dot{y}_1,\, \dot{x}_2,\, \dot{y}_2]^{\top},
\end{equation}
and the measurement $\mathbf{z} = [x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2]^{\top}$ is the box returned by the
detector. With a constant-velocity model, each frame the filter first predicts
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k-1} = F\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1}, \qquad
  P_{k|k-1} = F P_{k-1} F^{\top} + Q,
  \label{eq:kf_predict}
\end{equation}
where $F$ adds each velocity to its position, $P$ is the uncertainty of the estimate and
$Q$ the process noise. When a matching detection arrives, it corrects the prediction:
\begin{equation}
  K_k = P_{k|k-1} H^{\top}\left(H P_{k|k-1} H^{\top} + R\right)^{-1}, \quad
  \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k-1} + K_k\left(\mathbf{z}_k - H\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k-1}\right),
  \label{eq:kf_update}
\end{equation}
with $P_k = (I - K_k H) P_{k|k-1}$. Here $H$ selects the four corners from the state, $R$
is the measurement noise and $K_k$ the Kalman gain, which weighs the prediction against
the measurement according to their uncertainties.

\subsubsection{The assignment problem}

In every frame, $m$ predicted tracks must be matched with $n$ new detections. With a cost
$c_{ij} = -\mathrm{IoU}(\hat{b}_i, d_j)$ for track $i$ and detection $j$, the best
one-to-one matching minimises
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\pi} \sum_{(i,j) \in \pi} c_{ij},
  \label{eq:assign}
\end{equation}
which the Hungarian algorithm solves exactly. Matches whose IoU falls below a threshold
are rejected, leaving those tracks unmatched and those detections free to start new
tracks.

\subsubsection{Head pose from landmarks}

The Perspective-$n$-Point problem \cite{lepetit2009epnp} finds the rotation
$\mathbf{r}$ and translation $\mathbf{t}$ of a known 3D shape from the image positions of
its points. With 3D model points $\mathbf{P}_i^{3D}$ on a generic face and their observed
image positions $\mathbf{p}_i^{2D}$, the pose minimises the reprojection error
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{t}} \sum_{i=1}^{n}
  \left\| \mathbf{p}_i^{2D} - \pi\!\left(K,\, \mathbf{r},\, \mathbf{t},\, \mathbf{P}_i^{3D}\right) \right\|^2,
  \label{eq:pnp}
\end{equation}
where $\pi(\cdot)$ is perspective projection with the camera matrix $K$. The rotation
vector is converted to a rotation matrix with the Rodrigues formula, and the matrix is
decomposed into three Euler angles: pitch (nodding), yaw (turning left or right) and roll
(tilting).

\subsubsection{Gaussian blur}

A Gaussian blur replaces each pixel with a weighted average of its neighbours, using the
weights
\begin{equation}
  G(u, v) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2} \exp\!\left(-\frac{u^2 + v^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
  \label{eq:gauss}
\end{equation}
over a square kernel. The larger the kernel and $\sigma$, the more fine detail, such as
the shape of the eyes and mouth, is removed.

\subsection{System Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

The pipeline processes one frame at a time in five stages
(Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}). Each frame passes through detection, tracking, cue
extraction, classification and anonymization, and the output is the same frame with only
bystander faces blurred. Every decision is also written to a log, with the cue values that
led to it, which is what makes the later analyses possible.

Identity and appearance are kept separate. The tracker gives each face a number that only
says ``this is the same box as in the previous frame''; the cue stage looks at the face in
the current frame. No stage computes a face embedding or compares faces with each other,
so the system never learns who anyone is, and the track numbers are discarded when the
video ends.

\subsection{Face Detection}
\label{sec:detection}

YOLOv8n-face (Section~\ref{sec:lit_bg}) produces a bounding box and a confidence score for
every face. I chose it over BlazeFace after trying both on my videos: BlazeFace missed many
faces in scenes with five or more people and lost faces during fast head movement, while
YOLOv8n-face detected faces reliably in the crowded street scenes, with a model of about
6~MB. Each frame is scaled so that its longer side is 640 pixels before detection, and
overlapping boxes are merged by NMS. I set the confidence threshold to
$\tau_{\mathrm{det}} = 0.45$ and the NMS threshold to $\tau_{\mathrm{NMS}} = 0.45$ after
inspecting the output videos: lower values produced false detections on posters and
packaging, while higher values lost partly turned faces in dim indoor scenes. At most 20
faces are kept per frame.

\subsection{Multi-Face Tracking}
\label{sec:tracking}

Each frame, the tracker predicts every existing track with the Kalman filter, matches the
predictions to the new detections with the Hungarian algorithm, and accepts a match only if
the IoU is at least 0.25. Matched tracks are corrected with their detection, unmatched
detections start new tracks, and a track that receives no detection is kept for up to 90
frames (3~s at 30~fps), so that a face briefly hidden behind a hand or another person keeps
its number. If a track has been unmatched for more than five frames, its velocity is set to
zero, which stops the prediction drifting away while the face is hidden. A track is only
passed to the classifier once it has been matched in at least three frames, which filters
out one-frame false detections, and only in frames in which it was actually detected. No
appearance model of the face is ever built.

\subsection{Interaction Cues}
\label{sec:cues}

For each tracked face the system computes four cues. I developed them in two phases: the
two geometry cues first (Phase~2a), and the two landmark cues afterwards (Phase~2b).

\subsubsection{Geometry cues (Phase 2a)}

These cues use only the bounding box and follow from the centre bias of first-person
video: the wearer tends to face the person they are with, so that person appears large and
near the centre of the frame \cite{li2013gaze,fathi2012social}.

\textbf{Proximity.} For a face box $[x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2]$ in a $W \times H$ frame, the
area ratio is
\begin{equation}
  r_{\mathrm{area}} = \frac{(x_2 - x_1)(y_2 - y_1)}{W \cdot H}.
  \label{eq:area_ratio}
\end{equation}
In my videos, a person in conversation with the wearer typically produced values between
0.02 and 0.08, while distant bystanders were usually below 0.003. I set the threshold to
$\tau_{\mathrm{area}} = 0.012$ by looking at the distribution of each track's largest area
ratio across the videos.

\textbf{Centrality.} The distance of the face centre $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ from the frame
centre, normalised by the half-diagonal, is
\begin{equation}
  d_{\mathrm{center}} = \frac{\sqrt{(\bar{x} - W/2)^2 + (\bar{y} - H/2)^2}}
                             {\sqrt{(W/2)^2 + (H/2)^2}},
  \label{eq:center_dist}
\end{equation}
which is 0 at the centre and about 1 at the corners. A face counts as central when
$d_{\mathrm{center}} \le \tau_{\mathrm{center}} = 0.45$.

\subsubsection{Landmark cues (Phase 2b)}

For each tracked face, the box is enlarged by 15\% of its longer side so that the chin and
forehead are included, and the crop is passed to the MediaPipe Face Landmarker
\cite{kartynnik2019facemesh}, which returns 478 facial landmarks. Each crop is processed
on its own, since SORT already handles tracking. If no face is found in the crop, the
landmark cues are left empty for that frame and the classifier falls back to the geometry
cues.

\textbf{Head yaw.} I solve the PnP problem (Equation~\ref{eq:pnp}) for six landmarks with
known positions on a generic 3D face: the nose tip, the chin, the outer corners of both
eyes and both corners of the mouth (landmarks 4, 152, 33, 263, 61 and 291). The camera
matrix is approximated from the crop size,
\begin{equation}
  K = \begin{bmatrix} f & 0 & c_x \\ 0 & f & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix},
  \quad f = W_{\mathrm{crop}}, \quad c_x = W_{\mathrm{crop}}/2, \quad c_y = H_{\mathrm{crop}}/2,
  \label{eq:camera_matrix}
\end{equation}
and solved with OpenCV's iterative solver. The yaw angle $\theta_{\mathrm{yaw}}$ is the
cue; a face with $|\theta_{\mathrm{yaw}}| > \tau_{\mathrm{yaw}} = 40^\circ$ is treated as
looking away from the wearer.

\textbf{Mouth aspect ratio (MAR).} Following the eye aspect ratio of Soukupov\'a and
\v{C}ech \cite{soukupova2016}, I measure how far the mouth is open relative to its width.
The upper inner lip $\bar{u}$ is the mean of landmarks 82, 13 and 312, the lower inner lip
$\bar{l}$ the mean of 87, 14 and 317, and the mouth corners are landmarks 78 and 308:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MAR} = \frac{\lVert \bar{u} - \bar{l} \rVert}{\lVert p_{78} - p_{308} \rVert}.
  \label{eq:mar}
\end{equation}
MAR is close to zero when the mouth is closed. In my videos, values above about 0.08
corresponded to an open mouth during speech.

\subsection{Classification}
\label{sec:classification}

The classifier assigns each tracked face one of two labels in every frame:
\textsc{Interactor} (keep visible) or \textsc{Bystander} (blur). It follows three rules
of thumb: when in doubt, blur; never let one noisy frame change a label; and let the
landmark cues veto or support the geometry decision.

\subsubsection{Decision for a single frame}

The speaking test needs some history. For each track the system keeps the last
$N_w = 30$ MAR values and computes the fraction in which the mouth was open,
\begin{equation}
  s_{\mathrm{vad}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{W}|} \sum_{m \in \mathcal{W}}
                     \mathbf{1}\!\left[m \geq \tau_{\mathrm{MAR}}\right],
  \label{eq:vvad}
\end{equation}
once at least 10 values are available. A face that is speaking ($s_{\mathrm{vad}} \ge
\tau_{\mathrm{vad}} = 0.30$) and passes the proximity test counts as an interactor even if
it is slightly off-centre, up to $1.2\,\tau_{\mathrm{center}} = 0.54$. This covers a
partner who moves to the side while speaking.

\subsubsection{Confirmation and latching}

The single-frame decision is noisy, so a face's label only changes when the decision is
consistent (Figure~\ref{fig:state}). A face becomes an \textsc{Interactor} after the
single-frame decision says so in $\tau_{\mathrm{enter}} = 2$ consecutive frames, or at once
if it covers at least 4\% of the frame, because at that distance the person is almost
certainly face to face with the wearer. Once confirmed, the face is \emph{latched}: it
stays an interactor even if the cues later fail. Without the latch, a partner who looked
down at their cards or turned to someone else would be blurred and unblurred repeatedly,
which is distracting and would expose the face anyway on the frames it was visible. The
latch accepts a small risk to bystander privacy in exchange for stable partner
preservation. The latch lasts as long as the tracker reports the face; if the detector
misses the face in a frame, its state is cleared and it is confirmed again when it
reappears.

\subsection{Selective Anonymization and Baseline}
\label{sec:anon}

Every face labelled \textsc{Bystander} is blurred with a $51 \times 51$ Gaussian kernel
(Equation~\ref{eq:gauss}, $\sigma = 8$ pixels) over its box enlarged by 10\% on each side,
so that hair and ears are covered too. Faces labelled \textsc{Interactor} are left
untouched. The pipeline writes three videos from the same detections and tracks: the
selective output, a \emph{baseline} in which every detected face is blurred, and a debug
video that draws each decision. Because the baseline uses the same detector and tracker,
any difference between the two outputs comes from the classification stage alone.

\subsection{Offline Second Pass for Recorded Video}
\label{sec:secondpass}

The latch only works forward in time, so a partner is blurred until the system has seen
enough evidence. When the whole recording is available, two further rules can be applied
after the first pass, using only the decisions already in the log
(Figure~\ref{fig:secondpass}):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Retroactive latch.} A track confirmed as a partner at any point is kept
        visible for its whole time on screen, including the frames before confirmation.
  \item \textbf{Group rule.} A track that is visible for at least 10~seconds, and is on
        screen together with a confirmed partner for at least half of that time, is
        treated as part of the wearer's group and kept visible. Friends sitting at the same
        table stay in view for minutes, while passers-by rarely stay for 10~seconds.
        Requiring a confirmed partner to be present stops the rule from preserving
        strangers who simply stay in one place, such as a shop assistant.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{System decision for each data point}

For Sets A and B the system's decision for a track is \emph{partner} if the track was
confirmed as an interactor at any point in the video. For Set C the decision is what a
viewer of the output clip actually sees: a face counts as kept visible if it was unblurred
in at least half of the frames in which it was detected during the clip. I checked these
decisions against the rendered output videos by comparing each face region with the
original and the blanket-blurred frames, and all 104 matched.

\subsubsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

I treat \emph{interaction partner} as the positive class, so a true positive (TP) is a
partner kept visible, a false negative (FN) a partner blurred, a false positive (FP) a
bystander left visible, and a true negative (TN) a bystander blurred. The two main metrics
are
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{PSR} = \frac{TN}{TN + FP}, \qquad \mathrm{IPR} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}.
  \label{eq:psr_ipr}
\end{equation}
The Privacy Success Rate (PSR) is the share of bystanders who are blurred, and the
Interaction Preservation Rate (IPR) is the share of partners who stay visible. The names
are mine, but the measures follow BystandAR \cite{bystandar}, which reports the percentage
of bystanders protected and the percentage of intended subjects kept accessible. Using the
same two measures makes my results directly comparable with that system. In standard
classification terms, PSR is the specificity and IPR the recall (sensitivity) of the
partner class.

I report the two separately rather than a single accuracy figure for two reasons. The two
errors have different costs: a false positive exposes someone who did not consent, while a
false negative only removes useful content. And accuracy would be dominated by whichever
class is larger in a given sample. I also report precision, the share of kept faces that
really are partners, and the F$_1$ score, the harmonic mean of precision and IPR, for
comparison with general classification work. Every data point is a person rather than a
frame, because the question an annotator answers, and the decision the latch makes, is
about a person.

\subsubsection{Statistical analysis}
\label{sec:stats}

\textbf{Annotator agreement.} The ground truth is only useful if different people judge
the same faces the same way. Raw percentage agreement overstates this, because two
annotators who both label most faces as bystanders will agree often by chance. Cohen's
kappa corrects for chance agreement \cite{cohen1960}:
\begin{equation}
  \kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e},
  \label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
where $p_o$ is the observed agreement and $p_e$ the agreement expected if both annotators
labelled at random with their own label frequencies. $\kappa = 1$ means perfect agreement
and $\kappa = 0$ means no better than chance. Cohen's kappa compares two annotators, so I
computed it for every pair. To summarise agreement across all annotators at once, I used
Fleiss' kappa \cite{fleiss1971}, which extends the same idea to any fixed number of
annotators. I interpret values with the scale of Landis and Koch \cite{landis1977}: 0.41
to 0.60 is moderate, 0.61 to 0.80 substantial, and above 0.80 almost perfect agreement.

\textbf{Confidence intervals.} PSR and IPR are proportions estimated from samples of a few
dozen people, so each comes with uncertainty. For $k$ successes out of $n$ cases, with
$\hat{p} = k/n$ and $z = 1.96$, the 95\% Wilson score interval \cite{wilson1927} is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\hat{p} + \frac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}
       {1 + \frac{z^2}{n}}.
  \label{eq:wilson}
\end{equation}
I chose it over the more common normal-approximation interval because the latter fails
exactly where my results lie: for small samples and for proportions near 0\% or 100\%, it can
give intervals below 0\% or above 100\%, or of zero width when every case is correct. The
Wilson interval stays inside $[0, 1]$ and remains reliable for small samples.

\subsubsection{Ablation and sensitivity analysis}
\label{sec:replay}

Phase~2a and Phase~2b were first evaluated on different labelled sets, which makes it hard
to tell how much each cue contributes. Detection and tracking do not depend on the
classifier, and the log records every cue value the classifier used, so I can re-run the
classifier on the logged cues alone and obtain exactly the decisions a full re-run would
give. I used this to evaluate four variants on the same Set~C labels: geometry only, with
the yaw veto added, with the speaking boost added, and the full system. I also varied the
area threshold from 0.4\% to 2.0\% of the frame to see how the balance between PSR and IPR
depends on it. Before relying on the replay, I checked that it reproduces the logged
decisions.

\subsubsection{Speed and power measurement}

I measured processing time on the laptop using only the CPU. Each stage of the pipeline
was timed separately with a high-resolution timer over the first 300 frames of one
interaction video, and the throughput in frames per second (FPS) is the number of frames
divided by the total time. I ran the benchmark with the laptop on mains power and again on
battery, since wearable devices always run on battery and laptops limit processor speed
when unplugged.

\subsubsection{Projection to edge hardware}

I did not have a Raspberry Pi~5, so I estimated its performance from published
measurements. For the detection stage, which dominates the processing time, I used the
Raspberry Pi~5 benchmarks published by Ultralytics for the YOLOv8n model at the same
input size \cite{ultralytics_rpi}, in two export formats: PyTorch (the format my pipeline
uses) and NCNN, the fastest format available on that board. For the remaining stages,
which are ordinary CPU work, I applied a factor of 3.5. This factor comes from published
Geekbench~6 scores, in which the Raspberry Pi~5 scores roughly 2.5 to 3.5 times lower
than 13th-generation Intel laptop processors \cite{geekbench_pi5}; I used the upper end to
stay conservative.
