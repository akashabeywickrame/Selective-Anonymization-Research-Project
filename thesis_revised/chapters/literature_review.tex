\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:lit}

This chapter covers four areas that my work builds on: studies of how bystanders and
wearers feel about camera glasses, systems that protect bystanders in AR, first-person
(egocentric) vision, and lightweight face detection and tracking on edge hardware. It ends
by stating the research gap in Section~\ref{sec:gap}.

\subsection{Bystander Privacy in Augmented Reality}

Denning et al.\ ran 12 field sessions in cafes and interviewed 31 bystanders who had
been near someone wearing AR glasses \cite{denning2014}. Many were uncomfortable even when
the device was not recording, because they had no way to check what it was doing, and
they found existing measures such as physical lens covers or opt-out signals inadequate.
Two lessons from that study shaped my design: a privacy mechanism should not depend on
bystanders noticing it, and it should not require bystanders to do anything to be
protected.

O'Hagan et al.\ studied how bystanders' expectations change with context
\cite{ohagan2023}. People were far more concerned in professional and medical settings
than in casual public spaces, and the perceived intent of the wearer strongly affected
whether recording was acceptable. Their conclusion, that one fixed anonymization rule
cannot meet these varying needs, is the main motivation for making my system depend on
the social situation rather than blurring everyone.

The wearer's side has been studied too. Bhardwaj et al.\ found that camera-glasses users
often feel responsible for the people around them but consider the recording LED a poor
tool for meeting that responsibility \cite{bhardwaj2024}. Shao et al.\ surveyed 525
people and interviewed 20 wearer and bystander pairs \cite{mindthegap2026}. Bystanders
consistently wanted more transparency and protection than wearers were willing to give,
and in sensitive settings 65\% to 90\% said they would take defensive action. The authors
also compared twelve privacy-enhancing technologies and found that each was caught in at
least one of four trade-offs, one of which is protection versus utility. Blanket blurring
sits at the extreme protection end of that trade-off.

\subsection{Systems for Bystander Protection}

BystandAR by Corbett et al.\ is the closest system to mine \cite{bystandar}. It runs on a
Microsoft HoloLens~2 and decides who the wearer is engaging with by combining the wearer's
eye gaze, a directional microphone array focused on the wearer's speech, and spatial
mapping. In a 16-participant study it protected 98.14\% of bystanders while keeping
96.27\% of intended subjects visible, at 52.6 frames per second, with all processing on
the headset. The same group later argued that no current approach fully balances
protection, usability and consent in mixed reality \cite{corbett2024ieee}. BystandAR shows
that selective anonymization works well when the device knows where the wearer is looking.
The difficulty is that eye trackers and microphone arrays are found on high-end headsets,
not on everyday camera glasses.

BystandARIA by Al Aaraj takes a different route: a bystander carries a small LED device
that broadcasts a privacy request using visible light, and the AR system blurs that
person's face when it detects the signal \cite{bystandaria}. This gives bystanders real
control, but only those who carry the device are protected.

The remaining systems anonymize every face. EgoBlur, released by Meta with its Aria
research glasses, detects and blurs faces and licence plates in recorded first-person
video before the data is shared \cite{egoblur2023}. SafeAR integrates face detection and
blurring into an AR rendering pipeline without a large drop in frame rate
\cite{safear2025}, and SafeARUnity packages similar processing as middleware between the
camera and AR applications, evaluated in a location-based AR game \cite{safearunity2025}.
These systems show that face anonymization can be fast and practical, but none of them
can tell a companion from a stranger.

\subsection{First-Person Vision and Social Interaction}

First-person video differs from footage taken by a fixed camera. The camera moves with the
wearer's head, so there is frequent motion blur, quick changes of view, and large changes
in face size as the wearer approaches or moves away from people. One regularity is useful
for my problem: people tend to turn their head towards what they attend to, so the object
of attention usually sits near the centre of the frame. Li et al.\ measured this centre
bias directly when predicting gaze in egocentric video \cite{li2013gaze}.

Fathi et al.\ showed that social interactions can be detected from first-person video
alone \cite{fathi2012social}. They used the faces visible to the wearer, where those
faces were and which way they were looking, to recognise interaction types such as
dialogue and discussion. Their work supports the idea behind my cues: who the wearer is
interacting with leaves visible traces in the face positions and orientations in the
frame. Their method, however, classifies whole scenes offline and does not decide, face
by face, who should be anonymized.

Two further cues come from facial landmarks. Head pose can be estimated from a few
landmarks by solving the Perspective-$n$-Point problem \cite{lepetit2009epnp}; a person
whose head is turned well away from the camera is unlikely to be facing the wearer in
conversation. Mouth movement indicates speech. I measure it with a mouth aspect ratio,
adapted from the eye aspect ratio that Soukupov\'a and \v{C}ech used to detect blinks from
landmarks \cite{soukupova2016}. Tracking how often the mouth is open over a short window
gives a visual voice activity detector that needs no audio.

Khan et al.\ showed that compact deep learning models can recognise first-person context
directly on a wearable device without sending data to the cloud \cite{khan2021}. Their
task differs from mine, but it supports the choice to keep all processing on the device.

\subsection{Face Detection and Tracking on Edge Hardware}
\label{sec:lit_detect}

I considered three face detectors. Haar cascades are fast but miss faces that are turned,
small or blurred, which are common in first-person video. BlazeFace was designed for
sub-millisecond detection on mobile GPUs \cite{bazarevsky2019blazeface}, and together with
the MediaPipe face mesh it provides dense facial landmarks \cite{kartynnik2019facemesh}.
When I tried it on my videos, however, it missed many faces in scenes with five or more
people and lost faces during fast head movement, which is consistent with its design for
one or two close-up faces in video calls. I therefore chose YOLOv8n-face, a community
face-detection variant of the YOLOv8 nano model \cite{yolov8}. Its weights are about
6~MB and it detected faces reliably in the crowded street scenes of my dataset. I still use
MediaPipe, but only for landmarks on face crops that YOLO has already found.

Faces must keep the same identity from frame to frame so that cues can be accumulated over
time. SORT \cite{sort2016} associates detections across frames using a Kalman filter to
predict each box and the Hungarian algorithm to match predictions with new detections. It
uses box motion only, with no appearance features, so no face embedding is ever computed.
For a privacy system this matters as much as speed.

Karakostas et al.\ built a real-time wearable AR system for egocentric vision on edge
hardware and showed that multi-stage pipelines combining detection, tracking and context
inference can meet real-time limits on ARM processors when each stage is optimised for the
hardware \cite{karakostas2024}. Current camera glasses use chips such as the Qualcomm
Snapdragon AR1, which combines a small ARM CPU with a dedicated neural processing unit
\cite{qcom_ar1}. In this work I deliberately run everything on the CPU and treat the
Raspberry Pi~5, with four ARM Cortex-A76 cores, as a stand-in for the CPU of such devices.
Results obtained this way are a worst case: a deployed version could move the detector to
the neural processing unit.

\subsection{Privacy by Design}

My design follows the Privacy by Design principles \cite{cavoukian2009pbd}, especially
data minimisation: process only what the task needs and keep nothing longer than
necessary. This rules out face recognition entirely. The tracker gives each face a number
that exists only while the video is processed and is never linked to who the person is.
It also means anonymization should be selective. Blurring a partner the wearer is talking
to removes information without protecting anyone who needed protection, while recognising
and whitelisting known friends would build exactly the biometric database a privacy tool
should avoid.

First, systems that work with only a camera blur everyone, so they cannot preserve the
people the wearer is with. Second, the systems that are selective depend on hardware that
everyday camera glasses lack: an eye tracker and microphone array in the case of
BystandAR, and a device carried by each bystander in the case of BystandARIA. Third, the
first-person vision literature shows that face position, size and orientation carry
information about social interaction \cite{fathi2012social,li2013gaze}, but this has not
been used to decide which faces to anonymize.

The gap this research fills is therefore a selective anonymization method that separates
interaction partners from bystanders using only the RGB camera, without recognising
identities, and with computation light enough for wearable hardware. Because no labelled
dataset for this task exists, the research also needs an evaluation method that measures
bystander protection and partner preservation against human judgement.
