\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Camera glasses have moved out of research labs and into ordinary shops. Meta's Ray-Ban
glasses look like normal sunglasses but carry a 12-megapixel camera \cite{gsmarena_display}, and devices such as
the Apple Vision Pro and Snap Spectacles record the world from the wearer's eye level.
Large first-person video collections such as Ego4D \cite{grauman2022ego4d} show how much
of daily life these cameras capture: conversations at home, meals with friends, people
walking past in the street. That is what makes the devices useful for memory aids,
navigation, accessibility and translation. It is also what makes them a privacy problem,
because most of the people in that footage never chose to be recorded.

\subsection{Background and Motivation}

The people who appear in a wearer's video fall into two very different groups. Some are
\emph{interaction partners}: the friend across the table, the shopkeeper the wearer is
paying, the colleague in the meeting. They know they are with the wearer and are part of
the moment being recorded. Others are \emph{bystanders}: strangers walking past, people at
the next table, a crowd in a market. They have no relationship with the wearer and no
practical way to object. Denning et al.\ found that bystanders are uncomfortable around
camera glasses even when nothing is being recorded, because they cannot tell what the
device is doing \cite{denning2014}. Wearers feel this tension too: in interviews by
Bhardwaj et al., camera-glasses users said they felt responsible for the people around
them but had no good tool to protect them \cite{bhardwaj2024}.

Today there are two answers to this problem, and neither works well. The first is a small
LED that lights up while the camera records. It depends on bystanders noticing a tiny light,
often in daylight, and knowing what it means \cite{bhardwaj2024}. The second is to blur
every face, as Google Street View and Meta's EgoBlur tool do \cite{egoblur2023}. Blanket
blurring protects bystanders, but it also blurs the friend the wearer is talking to. For a
device whose purpose is to capture and assist social life, a recording in which every
companion is blurred has lost most of its value.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

What is missing is a way to treat the two groups differently: to blur bystanders and leave
interaction partners visible, automatically, and on the glasses themselves. Three
constraints make this hard.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No identity recognition.} The obvious solution, recognising the wearer's
        friends by their faces, needs a face recognition model and a database of known
        people. That creates exactly the kind of biometric record that a privacy tool
        should avoid.
  \item \textbf{No extra sensors.} BystandAR \cite{bystandar}, the closest existing system,
        separates partners from bystanders using the wearer's eye gaze and a directional
        microphone array. Most consumer glasses have neither.
  \item \textbf{Limited computing power.} Glasses run on small batteries and chips that sit
        next to the wearer's head. Any processing has to be light, and sending video to a
        server would itself expose bystanders' faces.
\end{itemize}

The problem I address in this thesis is therefore: \emph{how to decide, from a single
ordinary camera and without recognising anyone, which faces in first-person video belong
to people interacting with the wearer, and to blur only the others, using computation that
is realistic for wearable hardware.}

\subsection{Importance of the Problem}

If camera glasses become as common as smartphones, the number of people recorded without
consent will grow with them. Surveys of bystanders already show that between 65\% and 90\% would
take defensive action in sensitive settings \cite{mindthegap2026}, and a recent study concluded that a single fixed rule for every
situation cannot satisfy them; protection has to depend on context \cite{ohagan2023}. A
method that protects strangers while keeping the wearer's own social footage useful would
let these devices be used in public with far less harm. Because it would run on the device,
it would also avoid the alternative of sending every frame to a cloud service for
processing.

\subsection{Existing Approaches and the Research Gap}

Existing work falls into three groups, reviewed in Chapter~\ref{sec:lit}. Blanket
anonymization tools (EgoBlur, SafeAR, SafeARUnity) detect and blur every face, so they
protect bystanders but cannot keep interaction partners visible
\cite{egoblur2023,safear2025,safearunity2025}. Context-aware systems such as BystandAR do
separate the two groups, but rely on eye tracking and microphone arrays that most glasses
lack \cite{bystandar}. Consent-based schemes such as BystandARIA let bystanders signal
their wishes, but only if they carry a compatible device \cite{bystandaria}. No published
system separates partners from bystanders using only the RGB camera that every pair of
camera glasses already has. This is the gap my research addresses.

\subsection{Research Aim}

The aim of this research is to design, implement and evaluate a privacy pipeline that
blurs the faces of bystanders in first-person video while leaving the faces of the wearer's
interaction partners visible, using only a single RGB camera, without face recognition,
and with computation suitable for wearable-class hardware.

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rq}

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1.] Can a small set of visual cues taken from a single RGB camera (how large a
        face is, where it is in the frame, which way the head is turned, and whether the
        mouth is moving) separate a wearer's interaction partners from bystanders without
        recognising anyone's identity?
  \item[RQ2.] How well does selective anonymization based on these cues protect bystanders
        and keep interaction partners visible, compared with blurring every face?
  \item[RQ3.] How much computation does the pipeline need, and is it feasible on the CPU of
        wearable-class hardware?
\end{description}

\subsection{Research Objectives}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Build a face detection and tracking pipeline that keeps a stable, anonymous
        identity for each face in first-person video without storing any biometric data.
  \item Design interaction cues from face geometry, head pose and mouth movement, and a
        decision rule that combines them into a partner or bystander label for each face.
  \item Apply blurring only to faces labelled as bystanders, and produce a blanket-blurred
        version of the same video as a baseline.
  \item Assemble a dataset of first-person video and evaluate the system against human
        judgements, measuring bystander protection and partner preservation separately.
  \item Measure the processing time of each pipeline stage and estimate the performance on
        a Raspberry Pi~5 and on current camera-glasses hardware.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope}

I work only with the visual channel. The system does not use audio, depth sensing or eye
tracking, because most consumer glasses do not expose them to third-party software. The
decision rule is a hand-designed heuristic rather than a trained model, since no labelled
dataset of partners and bystanders existed when I started. I evaluate on recorded
first-person video collected from public online sources rather than footage recorded for
this study. Processing speed is measured on a laptop CPU and projected to
the Raspberry Pi~5, which I did not have access to.
