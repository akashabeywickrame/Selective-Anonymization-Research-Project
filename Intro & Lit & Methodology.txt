\section{Introduction}

Augmented Reality (AR) has undergone a fundamental transformation over the past decade,
evolving from laboratory prototypes into commercially available, always-on wearable
platforms that continuously overlay digital content onto the physical world. Consumer
devices such as the Meta Ray-Ban smart glasses and the Apple Vision Pro signal a shift
toward persistent egocentric capture: cameras mounted at eye level that record everything
a wearer sees, in real time, throughout the course of daily life \cite{ref:ar-evolution}.
This capability unlocks powerful assistive applications in healthcare, navigation,
education, and social communication. However, it simultaneously imposes a severe and
largely unresolved privacy burden on the people who appear in the wearer's field of
view bystanders who have neither consented to being recorded nor been given any
meaningful mechanism to prevent it.

The bystander privacy problem in AR is distinct from the general surveillance concerns
raised by smartphones or security cameras for two key reasons. First, egocentric cameras
are worn at face level, capturing high-resolution imagery of identifiable individuals at
close range during personal interactions. Second, the device is inherently mobile and
social: it follows the wearer into cafes, hospitals, classrooms, and homes precisely
the spaces where people hold the strongest expectation of privacy. Existing technical
countermeasures fail to adequately address this context. Blanket face blurring, as
deployed in applications such as Google Street View and broadcast news footage, treats
every detected face identically, making no distinction between a stranger passing in the
background and a close friend engaged in conversation with the wearer \cite{bystandar}.
This indiscriminate approach is doubly problematic: it degrades the social utility of
the AR device by obscuring the faces of people the wearer is actively interacting with,
while simultaneously imposing a continuous computational load that exceeds the power and
thermal budgets of resource-constrained wearable hardware. Visible LED indicators, the
alternative adopted by Meta Ray-Ban, rely on bystanders noticing a small light in
ambient conditions and understanding its meaning an assumption that is neither reliable
outdoors in bright sunlight nor informative to populations unfamiliar with the device
\cite{ref:bhardwaj2024}.

These limitations define a clear and actionable research gap: there is currently no
efficient, context-aware mechanism that selectively protects bystander privacy
while \emph{preserving} the utility of the device for the wearer. Addressing this gap
requires a framework that can, in real time and entirely on-device, distinguish between
people who are actively interacting with the wearer and those who are merely present in
the scene, and apply anonymization only to the latter group.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

The present effective technical solution to the bystander privacy issue is the blanket
face blurring used in applications such as Google Maps and news report footage, and the
LED indicator used in current consumer camera glasses (e.g., Meta Ray-Ban). However,
these solutions for bystander privacy are impractical for resource-constrained wearable
devices for several reasons:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Computational cost:} Continuous face detection and blurring
        significantly increases power consumption and heat generation near the
        user's head, reducing battery life and creating thermal discomfort.
  \item \textbf{Degraded user experience:} Indiscriminate blurring obscures socially
        relevant interactions, making the device unusable for its primary purpose of
        augmenting legitimate communication.
  \item \textbf{Lack of context awareness:} Existing systems make no distinction
        between a conversation partner standing one metre away and a stranger walking
        past in the background, resulting in both over-anonymization and
        under-anonymization depending on scene geometry.
  \item \textbf{Low visibility in bright light:} Outdoors in sunny conditions, an LED
        indicator may not be noticed by bystanders, rendering consent-based
        mechanisms ineffective.
\end{itemize}

Currently there is no efficient, context-aware mechanism that selectively protects
bystander privacy while preserving the utility of the device for the wearer.

\section{Literature Review}

This chapter surveys prior work across four interconnected domains that collectively
motivate and contextualise the proposed research: bystander privacy protection in
Augmented Reality systems; egocentric vision and interaction understanding; real-time
edge AI for visual inference; and privacy engineering principles that govern the system
design. The chapter concludes by synthesising the surveyed work into a structured
research gap that the proposed framework addresses.

\subsection{Bystander Privacy in Augmented Reality}

The emergence of always-on egocentric cameras has focused significant research attention
on the bystander privacy problem the challenge of protecting individuals who are
incidentally captured in a wearable camera's field of view without their knowledge or
consent. The foundational empirical investigation of this problem was conducted by
Denning et al., who performed 12 field sessions in cafes and interviewed 31 bystanders
about their reactions to co-located AR devices \cite{ref:denning2014}. Their study
revealed that bystanders held strong and largely unanticipated concerns about being
recorded, and that existing privacy-mediating technologies ranging from physical
covers to opt-out signals were perceived as inadequate. Critically, participants
expressed that the mere presence of an AR device induced discomfort regardless of
whether active recording was occurring, because there was no reliable mechanism for a
bystander to verify the device's state. This early finding established two design
requirements that persist in the present research: privacy mechanisms must be legible to
bystanders, and they must operate without requiring bystanders to take active steps to
protect themselves.

O'Hagan et al. extended this qualitative foundation by investigating how bystanders'
privacy expectations vary across social contexts in the era of commercially available AR
headsets \cite{ref:ohagan2023}. Through qualitative studies with participants drawn from
diverse demographic groups, the authors found that privacy preferences are highly
context-dependent: bystanders in professional or medical settings expressed
substantially stronger privacy concerns than those in casual public spaces, and the
perceived intent of the AR wearer significantly influenced whether recording was
considered acceptable. Critically, the authors conclude that static, universal
anonymization policies are insufficient effective bystander protection must be
context-sensitive and adaptive. This finding directly motivates the interaction-aware
approach taken in my research, which uses contextual visual cues rather than a
fixed blurring rule.

Building on these user-centred findings, a complementary strand of research has examined
the problem from the wearer's perspective. Bhardwaj et al. found that users of
camera-equipped glasses often feel personally responsible for protecting bystanders but
consider existing technical tools particularly LED indicators inadequate for
discharging this responsibility \cite{ref:bhardwaj2024}. Their CHI 2024 study
highlighted a social accountability gap: wearers want to signal their recording
behaviour but lack an effective, socially understood mechanism for doing so. A recent
large-scale multi-stakeholder study by Shao et al. reinforced and quantified this gap
using surveys of 525 participants and 20 paired interviews, finding that bystanders
consistently demand stronger information transparency and protective measures than
wearers are willing to provide, with these disparities intensifying sharply in sensitive
contexts where 65--90\% of bystanders reported they would take defensive action
\cite{ref:mindthegap2025}. The study further evaluated twelve privacy-enhancing
technologies and identified four fundamental trade-offs visibility versus unobtrusiveness,
automation versus user control, specificity versus generality, and protection versus
utility that undermine all current approaches and remain unresolved.

The most technically relevant prior work is BystandAR, proposed by Corbett et al.,
which represents the first practical on-device system for real-time bystander visual
data protection in AR \cite{bystandar}. BystandAR leverages eye gaze tracking,
wearer-focused directional microphones, and spatial awareness to distinguish intended
interaction partners from background bystanders. The system applies selective
anonymization to bystanders while preserving subjects the wearer is actively engaging
with. In an evaluation involving 16 participants, BystandAR correctly identified and
protected 98.14\% of bystanders while maintaining access to 96.27\% of intended
subjects, achieving a mean processing speed of 52.6 frames per second without any
off-device data transmission. The same research group subsequently published a broader
discussion of the design space in IEEE Security \& Privacy \cite{ref:corbett2024ieee},
examining the trade-offs between protection, usability, and consent in mixed reality
systems and arguing that no current solution adequately satisfies all three requirements.
These results establish a strong performance benchmark for my research.
However, BystandAR relies on dedicated hardware specifically, an eye gaze tracker and
directional microphone array that is not present on commodity AR glasses or edge
devices such as the Raspberry Pi 5. My research addresses this limitation by
achieving comparable selective anonymization using only a monocular RGB camera and
computationally inexpensive visual cues.

Al Aaraj et al. proposed BystandARIA, a complementary approach that uses visible light
communication (VLC) via LEDs to allow bystanders to actively signal their privacy
preferences to an AR device \cite{ref:bystandaria}. A bystander carrying a VLC-enabled
device can broadcast a privacy request that the AR system detects and responds to by
blurring the corresponding face. BystandARIA is notable because it shifts some agency to
the bystander, partially addressing the consent limitation of automated systems. However,
it requires bystanders to carry compatible hardware and is therefore unsuitable as a
general-purpose solution. The present research treats automated visual classification as
the primary mechanism, with bystander-controlled signalling identified as a potential
complementary future extension.

Meta Reality Labs introduced EgoBlur, a privacy-preserving pipeline for head-mounted
egocentric video that combines automated face and licence plate detection with blurring
\cite{egoblur2023}. EgoBlur operates as a post-processing tool applied to recorded video
rather than a real-time on-device system, and it performs blanket anonymization of all
detected faces without context awareness. While EgoBlur demonstrates the engineering
feasibility of large-scale egocentric anonymization, its non-selective approach and
offline processing model place it outside the scope of the present research's target use
case.

\subsection{Real-Time Privacy Protection in AR Applications}

A parallel strand of research has focused on integrating privacy protection directly
into AR application middleware and rendering pipelines, rather than treating
anonymization as a separate post-processing step. SafeARUnity, developed by Ribeiro et
al., introduces a real-time image processing middleware library that acts as a layer
between an AR application and the device camera, identifying and sanitizing sensitive
visual data prior to rendering \cite{ref:safearunity2025}. The system was designed for
and evaluated in the context of Location-Based AR Games (LBARGs), using a purpose-built
game called LootAR as the evaluation platform. SafeARUnity demonstrates that privacy
protection can be embedded into the AR rendering pipeline with acceptable performance
overhead on mobile hardware, and that a middleware approach allows privacy logic to be
applied across multiple AR applications without modification to each application's
codebase. However, like EgoBlur and SafeAR, the SafeARUnity system applies blanket
anonymization without interaction-aware classification: all detected sensitive data is
sanitized regardless of social context or the wearer's intent. This limitation is
precisely the gap that the present research addresses by adding a context-aware decision
layer between detection and anonymization.

Costa et al. proposed SafeAR, a system architecture for privacy maintenance in AR
applications that provides face detection and blurring integrated into an AR rendering
pipeline without unacceptable frame rate degradation \cite{ref:safear2025}. SafeAR
demonstrates that privacy-preserving processing can be incorporated into production AR
systems without prohibitive latency, establishing an architectural precedent for the
pipeline approach adopted in the present research. The present work extends this line of
research by replacing the blanket blurring decision with a context-sensitive
classification layer, addressing the utility degradation identified as a core limitation
of non-selective approaches.

\subsection{Egocentric Vision and Interaction Understanding}

Egocentric vision the analysis of video captured from a first-person, head-mounted
perspective presents unique challenges compared to third-person surveillance or
broadcast video. The camera undergoes continuous ego-motion tied to the wearer's head
movements, producing frequent motion blur, rapid viewpoint changes, and significant
variation in face scale as the wearer moves toward or away from other people. Faces of
interaction partners appear large and centrally located, while bystanders appear smaller
and peripherally distributed a spatial regularity known as center bias that has been
extensively documented in egocentric attention research and forms a foundational
assumption of the present work.

Khan et al. demonstrated that computationally intensive activity recognition tasks can
be performed directly on resource-constrained wearable hardware using compact on-device
deep learning models, without requiring cloud offloading \cite{ref:khan2021}. Their
system achieves personalised, privacy-preserving egocentric context detection on a
wearable platform, establishing the general feasibility of on-device inference for the
class of problems addressed by this research. While their focus is on activity
recognition rather than face-level interaction detection, the architectural principles lightweight models, local inference, and avoidance of biometric data transmission
directly inform the design philosophy of the present framework.

The use of Visual Voice Activity Detection (V-VAD) as an interaction cue represents a
particularly relevant thread of prior work. V-VAD estimates speech activity from visual
observation of lip and mouth movement, without requiring audio input. In the context of
egocentric video, a face with high-frequency temporal variation in its mouth aspect ratio
(MAR) is highly likely to be engaged in speech directed at the wearer. The present
research operationalises V-VAD as one of three complementary interaction cues alongside
proximity and head pose estimation, fusing them in a heuristic decision layer that
requires no learned parameters and is therefore directly deployable on CPU-only edge
hardware.

Head pose estimation from facial landmarks provides a complementary interaction cue: a
face with a yaw angle exceeding approximately 45 degrees from the frontal plane is
oriented away from the camera and therefore almost certainly not engaged in direct
interaction with the wearer. Landmark-based head pose estimation is a well-established
technique that can be performed in real time using the output of a face detection model
without requiring a dedicated pose estimation network, making it well-suited to the
computational constraints of the target hardware.

\subsection{Face Detection and Tracking on Edge Devices}

The choice of face detection architecture is a critical design decision for a
resource-constrained AR privacy system. Three architectures are relevant to the present
research: BlazeFace, YOLOv8n-face, and Haar Cascade detectors.

BlazeFace, the detection backbone of MediaPipe Face Mesh, was designed by Google
specifically for sub-millisecond inference on mobile ARM processors
\cite{ref:karakostas2024}. It uses a lightweight single-shot detector with an anchor
scheme optimised for the distribution of face sizes in mobile camera imagery, and
outputs 468 dense 3D facial landmarks alongside the bounding box. These landmarks
enable head pose estimation and V-VAD computation without a second cascaded model, which
is a significant computational advantage. However, empirical evaluation conducted during
the preliminary phase of the present research revealed a critical limitation: BlazeFace
exhibits substantially reduced detection recall in crowded scenes containing five or more
faces, and loses tracking stability under fast motion and partial occlusion. This is
consistent with the architecture's design target of one to two close-up faces, as
typical in mobile video calling applications, and renders it unsuitable for the
multi-person, dynamic crowd scenes characteristic of egocentric video in public
environments.

YOLOv8n-face, the nano variant of the YOLOv8 face detection model, was selected as the
detection backbone for the present research following this empirical comparison.
YOLOv8n-face is a community-trained adaptation of the YOLOv8n object detector fine-tuned
on face detection datasets. With a model weight file of approximately 6 megabytes and
support for ONNX export enabling CPU-only inference on ARM hardware, it provides a
favourable balance between detection accuracy in multi-person scenes and computational
cost. Critically, it maintains reliable detection recall in crowds of five or more faces
under fast motion a requirement confirmed during preliminary testing on the bystander
and interaction video datasets used in this research.

Multi-face tracking is equally important as detection for the present framework: stable,
persistent face identities across frames are required for the interaction cue extraction
stage to accumulate temporal evidence (e.g., MAR variance over 30 frames for V-VAD).
SORT (Simple Online and Realtime Tracking), the tracking algorithm used in the present
implementation, combines Kalman filter-based motion prediction with Hungarian algorithm
assignment to associate detections across frames. SORT is motion-onlyit does not
require a face embedding or re-identification model which is a significant advantage
for a privacy-preserving system that must not generate or store biometric face
representations.

\subsection{Real-Time AR Systems on Edge Hardware}

Karakostas et al. demonstrated a real-time wearable AR system for egocentric vision
deployed on edge hardware, providing a comprehensive analysis of the architectural and
optimization strategies required to achieve low-latency visual inference on ARM-class
processors \cite{ref:karakostas2024}. Their work confirms that complex multi-stage
visual pipelines including object detection, tracking, and context inference can be
executed in real time on resource-constrained devices provided that each stage is
individually optimized for the target hardware's instruction set and memory hierarchy.
This finding supports the architectural decomposition adopted in the present research, in
which each pipeline stage detection, tracking, cue extraction, classification, and
anonymization is implemented as an independently optimized module.

The Raspberry Pi 5, equipped with a quad-core ARM Cortex-A76 processor running at 2.4
GHz and 8 GB of LPDDR4X memory, represents the target deployment hardware for the
proposed pipeline. The ARM Cortex-A76 micro-architecture is instruction-set compatible
with the application processors used in Qualcomm Snapdragon XR2 and similar wearable
SoCs, making it a valid proxy for expected performance on commercial AR hardware. By
intentionally foregoing GPU or NPU acceleration and targeting a general-purpose CPU, the
present research adopts a conservative, worst-case evaluation strategy.

As direct access to the Raspberry Pi~5 hardware was not available during the evaluation
phase of this research, pipeline performance benchmarks are obtained on a desktop-class
laptop CPU (Intel Core i-series). Raspberry Pi~5 performance is then projected by
applying a hardware scaling factor derived from published inference benchmarks comparing
Intel Core-class and ARM Cortex-A76-class processors on equivalent YOLO workloads. This
methodology is consistent with established practice in edge AI research when the target
hardware is unavailable for direct measurement \cite{ref:karakostas2024}.

\subsection{Privacy Engineering and Privacy by Design}

The system architecture of the present research is grounded in the Privacy by Design
(PbD) framework, which advocates for embedding privacy protections into system design
from the outset rather than retrofitting them as compliance measures. The most relevant
PbD principle for this work is Data Minimization: the system should collect and process
only the personal data strictly necessary for its function, and should avoid generating
or retaining any data beyond what is required in the moment of processing. This principle
directly excludes face recognition and biometric embedding from the design space not
merely as a performance optimization, but as a fundamental privacy commitment. The
proposed framework assigns temporary, session-scoped numeric identities to tracked faces
purely for the purpose of accumulating temporal interaction evidence within a single
video stream; these identities are not linked to any persistent biometric representation
and are discarded at the end of each session.

The principle of Selective, Minimal Anonymization follows from Data Minimization: only
the visual data that poses a genuine privacy risk the faces of non-interacting
bystanders are obfuscated. The faces of active interaction partners are preserved,
because obfuscating them would constitute an unnecessary degradation of legitimately
useful information. This approach contrasts with blanket blurring systems, which
over-anonymize by treating interaction partners as equivalent to bystanders, and with
face recognition systems, which under-anonymize by attempting to whitelist known
individuals at the cost of creating a biometric database.

\section{Methodology}

This chapter presents the design and implementation of my proposed context-aware
privacy framework. The framework is structured as a five-stage sequential pipeline that
processes each frame of an egocentric video stream: face detection, multi-face tracking,
interaction cue extraction, context-aware classification, and selective anonymization.
The chapter concludes with a description
of the blanket-blurring baseline used for comparative evaluation and the metrics used to
assess performance.

\subsection{System Architecture}

The proposed system is organised as a modular, single-pass pipeline that processes
one video frame at a time. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} illustrates the data flow. A raw
BGR frame is passed sequentially through the detection, tracking, cue extraction,
classification, and anonymization stages; the output is an anonymized frame in which
only bystander faces are blurred, while interaction partner faces remain unmodified.

A key architectural property is the separation of \emph{identity management} from
\emph{appearance analysis}. The tracking stage assigns and maintains stable numeric
identifiers for each face across frames; the cue extraction stage operates on the
current frame content given those identifiers. This separation means that no biometric
embedding or face recognition model is required at any stage. The numeric identifiers
are session-scoped and ephemeral, consistent with the Data Minimization principle
discussed in Section~2.6.

All components are implemented in Python 3.11 using OpenCV 4.x for frame I/O and
image operations, Ultralytics YOLOv8 for face detection, a custom SORT implementation
for tracking, and MediaPipe 0.10 for facial landmark extraction. The pipeline is
designed to execute on a single CPU core with no GPU or NPU dependency, targeting
edge CPU hardware without hardware acceleration. Performance is benchmarked on a
laptop CPU and Raspberry Pi~5 throughput is estimated via a hardware scaling factor
as described in Section~2.5.

\subsection{Face Detection}

The first stage detects all faces present in each frame and produces axis-aligned
bounding boxes and detection confidence scores. Based on the empirical comparison
described in Section~2.4, YOLOv8n-face was selected as the detection backbone.
YOLOv8n-face is the nano variant of the YOLOv8 architecture fine-tuned on face
detection datasets, with a model weight file of approximately 6~MB. It operates as a
single-shot detector: the full frame is resized to a fixed input resolution of
$640 \times 640$ pixels, passed through the network, and non-maximum suppression (NMS)
is applied to the raw anchor outputs to produce the final set of detections.

A minimum confidence threshold of $\tau_{\mathrm{det}} = 0.45$ and an NMS IoU threshold
of $\tau_{\mathrm{NMS}} = 0.45$ were selected empirically to suppress spurious low-confidence
detections without discarding valid faces in partially occluded or poorly lit conditions.
A maximum of 20 simultaneous faces are tracked per frame, a limit that comfortably
exceeds the face count expected in typical egocentric interaction scenarios.

\subsection{Multi-Face Tracking}

The tracking stage associates the set of detections from the current frame with the set
of active tracks from the previous frame, maintaining a stable numeric \texttt{track\_id}
for each face across consecutive frames. Stable identities are essential for the
interaction cue extraction stage, which accumulates temporal evidence such as mouth
aspect ratio variance over a 30-frame window; without tracking, each frame's detections
would be anonymous and no per-person history could be maintained.

SORT (Simple Online and Realtime Tracking) is used for this purpose. SORT
models each face's bounding box trajectory as a linear Kalman filter and uses the
Hungarian algorithm to solve the bipartite matching problem between the predicted
positions of existing tracks and the new detections. The IoU (Intersection over Union) between predicted and
detected bounding boxes serves as the matching cost. Tracks that fail to receive a
matching detection are retained in a ``lost'' state for up to 90 frames (3~seconds at
30~fps) before being discarded, which prevents identity switches when a face is
temporarily occluded. A track is reported as confirmed only after receiving at least 3
consecutive detections, which prevents transient false detections from entering the
classification stage.

SORT is a motion-only tracker: it requires no face embedding, identity descriptor, or
appearance model. This is a critical property for the present system, as any
re-identification model that encodes facial appearance would constitute biometric
processing. The Kalman filter parameters use velocity-and-position state estimation with
a constant-velocity motion model, which is adequate for the relatively smooth,
continuous motion of faces in egocentric video at normal walking pace.

\subsection{Interaction Cue Extraction}

The interaction cue extraction stage computes four scalar features for each tracked face
that collectively characterise whether the face belongs to an active interaction partner
or a background bystander. The four cues are divided into two groups based on the
information they require.

\subsubsection*{Phase 2a Cues: Bounding-Box Geometry}

The first two cues are derived entirely from the bounding box produced by the detector
and require no additional computation. They exploit the center bias of egocentric video:
the camera wearer naturally orients their head toward their interlocutor, so an active
conversation partner's face tends to be large and centrally located in the frame.

\textbf{Proximity} (area ratio). Let $[x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2]$ denote the bounding box of
a tracked face, and let $W \times H$ be the frame dimensions. The face area ratio is
defined as:
\begin{equation}
  r_{\mathrm{area}} = \frac{(x_2 - x_1)(y_2 - y_1)}{W \cdot H}
  \label{eq:area_ratio}
\end{equation}
$r_{\mathrm{area}}$ captures how much of the frame the face occupies. A close conversation
partner produces values in the range $0.02$ to $0.08$; a distant bystander produces
values below $0.003$. A threshold of $\tau_{\mathrm{area}} = 0.012$ separates these two
regimes, calibrated from the empirical distribution of per-track maximum area ratios
observed across the evaluation videos.

\textbf{Centrality} (center distance). The normalized distance of the face center from
the optical center of the frame is defined as:
\begin{equation}
  d_{\mathrm{center}} = \frac{\sqrt{(\bar{x} - W/2)^2 + (\bar{y} - H/2)^2}}
                             {\sqrt{(W/2)^2 + (H/2)^2}}
  \label{eq:center_dist}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{x} = (x_1 + x_2)/2$ and $\bar{y} = (y_1 + y_2)/2$ are the face center
coordinates. The denominator normalizes by the half-diagonal of the frame so that
$d_{\mathrm{center}} = 0$ at the frame center and $d_{\mathrm{center}} \approx 1$ at the
corners. A threshold of $\tau_{\mathrm{center}} = 0.45$ is used: faces within 45\% of
the frame center along the normalized diagonal are considered sufficiently central.

\subsubsection*{Phase 2b Cues: Facial Landmark Analysis}

The second two cues require the positions of specific facial landmarks, which are
extracted using MediaPipe Face Mesh. For each tracked face, the bounding box produced
by SORT is padded by 15\% on each side to ensure that the chin and forehead are
captured, and the resulting crop is passed to MediaPipe Face Mesh in
\texttt{static\_image\_mode} (i.e., each crop is treated independently, with no temporal
state maintained by Face Mesh itself). Face Mesh returns a set of 468 3D landmark
coordinates normalized to the crop dimensions. If Face Mesh fails to detect a face
within the crop, the Phase 2b cues are set to \texttt{None} and the classification
stage reverts to Phase 2a cues only for that track in that frame.

\textbf{Head-pose yaw} (orientation cue). Head-pose estimation is performed by solving
the Perspective-$n$-Point (PnP) problem using six key facial landmarks
whose positions in a canonical 3D face model are known: the nose tip, chin, left and
right eye outer corners, and left and right mouth corners (MediaPipe landmark indices
4, 152, 33, 263, 61, and 291 respectively). Let
$\mathbf{P}_i^{3D} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ denote the $i$-th 3D model point and
$\mathbf{p}_i^{2D} \in \mathbb{R}^2$ its corresponding image point in the crop.
The camera intrinsic matrix is approximated as:
\begin{equation}
  K = \begin{bmatrix} f & 0 & c_x \\ 0 & f & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix},
  \quad f = W_{\mathrm{crop}}, \quad c_x = W_{\mathrm{crop}}/2, \quad c_y = H_{\mathrm{crop}}/2
  \label{eq:camera_matrix}
\end{equation}
where $W_{\mathrm{crop}} \times H_{\mathrm{crop}}$ are the crop dimensions and $f$ is
the approximate focal length set equal to the crop width. The iterative PnP solver minimizes the sum of squared reprojection errors:
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{t}} \sum_{i=1}^{6}
  \left\| \mathbf{p}_i^{2D} - \pi\!\left(K,\, \mathbf{r},\, \mathbf{t},\, \mathbf{P}_i^{3D}\right) \right\|^2
  \label{eq:pnp}
\end{equation}
where $\pi(\cdot)$ denotes the perspective projection. The resulting rotation vector
$\mathbf{r}$ is converted to a $3\times3$ rotation matrix $R$ via the Rodrigues formula,
and the RQ decomposition of $R$ yields the Euler angles $(\alpha, \beta, \gamma)$
representing pitch, yaw, and roll respectively. The yaw angle $\beta$ (rotation about
the vertical axis) is used as the orientation cue:
\begin{equation}
  \theta_{\mathrm{yaw}} = \beta \quad [\text{degrees}]
  \label{eq:yaw}
\end{equation}
A face with $|\theta_{\mathrm{yaw}}| > \tau_{\mathrm{yaw}} = 40^\circ$ is considered to
be looking away from the camera wearer and is therefore classified as a bystander
regardless of proximity or centrality.

\textbf{Mouth Aspect Ratio} (V-VAD cue). The Mouth Aspect Ratio (MAR) quantifies the
degree to which the mouth is open and is used as a visual proxy for speech activity.
Using the inner lip landmarks from MediaPipe Face Mesh, three upper inner lip points
(indices 82, 13, 312) and three lower inner lip points (indices 87, 14, 317) are
averaged to obtain representative upper ($\bar{u}$) and lower ($\bar{l}$) lip
positions. The left and right inner lip corners are taken from indices 78 and 308. The
MAR is then:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MAR} = \frac{\lVert \bar{u} - \bar{l} \rVert}
                      {\lVert p_{78} - p_{308} \rVert}
  \label{eq:mar}
\end{equation}
where $\lVert \cdot \rVert$ denotes the Euclidean norm. MAR approaches zero when the
mouth is closed; values above approximately 0.08 correspond to a mouth open sufficiently
to indicate speech.

\subsection{Context-Aware Classification}

The classification stage maps the four cues computed for a given tracked face in a given
frame to a binary label: \textsc{Interactor} (preserve) or \textsc{Bystander}
(anonymize). The design follows three principles: (i) \emph{bystander by default}, so
that uncertain cases are treated as bystanders to maximise privacy protection; (ii)
\emph{temporal hysteresis}, so that a single noisy frame cannot flip a face's label;
and (iii) \emph{landmark cues as veto and boost}, so that the orientation and V-VAD cues
can strengthen or override the geometric Phase 2a decision.

\subsubsection*{Instantaneous Decision Function}

For each frame, the instantaneous decision function $f$ maps the current cue vector to a
tentative label. The function is evaluated in the following priority order:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Yaw veto.} If landmark data is available and
        $|\theta_{\mathrm{yaw}}| > 40^\circ$, return \textsc{Bystander} immediately.
        A face turned significantly away from the camera cannot be in direct face-to-face
        interaction with the wearer.

  \item \textbf{Proximity and centrality.} Evaluate the Phase 2a rule:
        \begin{equation}
          f_{\mathrm{geo}} = \bigl[r_{\mathrm{area}} \geq \tau_{\mathrm{area}}\bigr]
                             \wedge
                             \bigl[d_{\mathrm{center}} \leq \tau_{\mathrm{center}}\bigr]
          \label{eq:geo_rule}
        \end{equation}
        If $f_{\mathrm{geo}}$ is true, return \textsc{Interactor} (subject to V-VAD
        boost below).

  \item \textbf{V-VAD boost.} The V-VAD score is computed as the fraction of frames in
        the rolling window with $\mathrm{MAR} \geq \tau_{\mathrm{MAR}} = 0.08$:
        \begin{equation}
          s_{\mathrm{vad}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{W}|} \sum_{m \in \mathcal{W}}
                             \mathbf{1}\!\left[m \geq \tau_{\mathrm{MAR}}\right]
          \label{eq:vvad}
        \end{equation}
        where $\mathcal{W}$ is the set of MAR values currently in the history deque.
        If $s_{\mathrm{vad}} \geq \tau_{\mathrm{vad}} = 0.30$ and
        $r_{\mathrm{area}} \geq \tau_{\mathrm{area}}$, the face is classified as
        \textsc{Interactor} even if $d_{\mathrm{center}} > \tau_{\mathrm{center}}$
        (with the centrality threshold relaxed to $1.2 \cdot \tau_{\mathrm{center}}$).
        This handles the case where an active conversation partner has drifted slightly
        off the optical center during animated speech.

  \item \textbf{Default.} If none of the above conditions is satisfied, return
        \textsc{Bystander}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Temporal Hysteresis and Latching}

The instantaneous label returned by $f$ updates the enter or exit streak for the track.
A label transition from \textsc{Bystander} to \textsc{Interactor} is committed only
when the enter streak reaches $\tau_{\mathrm{enter}} = 2$ consecutive frames. A special
case applies: if $r_{\mathrm{area}} \geq 0.04$ (a face covering at least 4\% of the
frame), the transition is committed immediately regardless of the streak, since such
proximity is an unambiguous indicator of direct face-to-face engagement.

Once a track has been committed as \textsc{Interactor}, the label is \emph{latched}: it
is not reverted to \textsc{Bystander} even if the instantaneous cues subsequently fail.
This design choice prevents flickering between labels when a conversation partner
momentarily looks away or is partially occluded, and is consistent with the observation
that once a direct interaction has been established the wearer's engagement partner
remains socially present throughout the interaction. Latching represents a deliberate
trade-off in favour of interaction preservation rate (IPR) over privacy success rate
(PSR), reflecting the system's utility objective.

\subsection{Selective Anonymization}

For each frame, all faces classified as \textsc{Bystander} are anonymized by applying
a Gaussian blur to the face region. The bounding box is first expanded by a padding
factor of 10\% on each side to ensure that hair and ears are covered in addition to the
central face region. A Gaussian kernel of size $51 \times 51$ pixels with a
correspondingly large standard deviation is applied to the padded region, which
produces a smooth, visually consistent blur that eliminates identifiable facial features
while preserving the presence and approximate shape of the person in the scene. Faces
classified as \textsc{Interactor} are passed to the output frame unmodified.

The selective output is produced alongside two reference streams: a \emph{blanket-blur}
stream in which all detected faces are blurred regardless of classification (the
baseline), and a \emph{debug} stream in which bounding boxes are annotated in green
(\textsc{Interactor}) or red (\textsc{Bystander}) to facilitate visual inspection of
the classification decisions.

\subsection{Baseline: Blanket-Blurring}

The baseline system applies identical Gaussian blur to every detected face in every frame,
making no use of the interaction cues. This corresponds to the current state-of-the-art
approach used in applications such as Google Street View and EgoBlur. The baseline uses
the same YOLOv8n-face detector and SORT tracker as the proposed system, ensuring that any
performance difference is attributable solely to the selective classification layer rather
than to differences in the underlying detection quality.

The proposed system is designed to outperform the baseline on two dimensions: the
Interaction Preservation Rate, which the baseline degrades to zero (all partners are
blurred), and the per-frame computational load, since selective anonymization skips the
Gaussian convolution for faces classified as \textsc{Interactor}.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

The performance of the proposed system is evaluated using two primary task-level metrics,
supplemented by computational efficiency metrics.

\textbf{Privacy Success Rate (PSR)} measures the proportion of bystander faces that
are correctly anonymized:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{PSR} = \frac{TP_B}{TP_B + FN_B}
  \label{eq:psr}
\end{equation}
where $TP_B$ is the number of bystander face-frames correctly blurred and $FN_B$ is the
number of bystander face-frames incorrectly left unblurred. PSR captures the system's
ability to protect bystander privacy.

\textbf{Interaction Preservation Rate (IPR)} measures the proportion of interaction
partner faces that are correctly left unblurred:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{IPR} = \frac{TP_I}{TP_I + FN_I}
  \label{eq:ipr}
\end{equation}
where $TP_I$ is the number of partner face-frames correctly preserved and $FN_I$ is the
number of partner face-frames incorrectly blurred. IPR captures the system's utility
preservation.

Ground-truth labels (bystander or interaction partner, per face per frame) are
established by manual annotation of the evaluation video clips using a purpose-built
annotation tool. Both metrics are reported across all evaluation clips and separately for
the bystander-only and interaction video subsets, enabling analysis of false-positive
(over-anonymization) and false-negative (under-anonymization) failure modes.

\textbf{Computational efficiency} is reported as average frames per second (FPS) over
the full evaluation set, and as per-stage latency (in milliseconds) measured using
high-resolution wall-clock timing (\texttt{time.perf\_counter}). These measurements are
conducted on a desktop CPU to establish a baseline, with Raspberry Pi~5 performance
estimated by applying a hardware scaling factor derived from published ARM
Cortex-A76 inference benchmarks.

\subsection{Dataset and Ground-Truth Annotation}
\label{sec:dataset}

The evaluation dataset comprises 21 short video clips captured using a smartphone
held at approximately eye level to simulate the egocentric perspective of a
wearable camera. The clips are divided into two categories: 14 \emph{interaction}
clips, in which the camera wearer conducts a face-to-face conversation with at
least one person, and 7 \emph{bystander} clips, in which no direct interaction
takes place and all visible faces belong to people who are present but not
conversing with the wearer. All clips were recorded indoors in a university
environment under standard artificial lighting. Clip durations range from
approximately 10 to 60 seconds, capturing a total of 66 unique face tracks after
SORT-based tracking.

each clip. A track was assigned the label \emph{interaction partner} (1) if the

binary protocol is consistent with the INTERACT\_LATCH behaviour of the classifier,
which permanently commits a track to the \emph{Interactor} state once the
interaction criteria are met.

hardware cost relative to the baseline, since both configurations share the same
YOLOv8n-face detector and SORT tracker.

\subsection{Computational Performance}
\label{sec:fps}

Pipeline latency was measured by re-running the full processing loop on a desktop
CPU (Intel Core i-series, no GPU or NPU acceleration enabled) and timing each stage
independently using \texttt{time.perf\_counter} over a 300-frame clip. Table~\ref{tab:fps}
reports the per-stage mean latency for the Phase~2a configuration.

Face detection with YOLOv8n-face dominates the pipeline at 55.1~ms per frame
(64.1\% of total latency). The classification stage is negligible at 0.02~ms,
confirming that the heuristic decision layer imposes no measurable overhead relative
to the underlying detection and tracking stages.

\paragraph{Raspberry Pi 5 projection.}
Direct benchmarking on a Raspberry Pi 5 was not conducted, as the target hardware
was not available at the time of evaluation. Following the methodology described in
Section~3.8, a conservative hardware scaling factor of 3.5$\times$ is applied to
project performance on the ARM Cortex-A76 processor of the Raspberry Pi 5. This
factor is derived from published YOLO inference benchmarks comparing Intel Core-class
and ARM Cortex-A76 class processors on equivalent workloads. Applying this factor to
the measured 86.0~ms per-frame latency yields an estimated throughput of
approximately \textbf{3.3 FPS} on the Raspberry Pi 5 for the Phase~2a configuration.
With facial landmark extraction enabled (Phase~2b), where MediaPipe Face Landmarker
adds approximately 15--25~ms per face crop, the estimated throughput reduces to
approximately 2--3~FPS on the same hardware.

These results confirm that the proposed pipeline, while operating in real time on
desktop-class hardware, falls below the 30-FPS threshold on the Raspberry Pi 5 in
its current form. Section~5.3 discusses optimisation strategies that could bring
edge-device throughput closer to real time.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This chapter interprets the experimental results in the context of the research
questions posed in Chapter~1, examines the failure modes of the proposed system,
compares performance with the most relevant prior work, and identifies the
limitations of the current evaluation.

\subsection{Classification Performance and Research Question}
\label{sec:disc_perf}

The central research question asked whether a context-aware, multi-cue heuristic
operating on geometry and facial landmarks could selectively anonymize bystanders
while preserving interaction partners, without biometric recognition or cloud
offloading. The Phase~2a evaluation results (PSR = 87.50\%, IPR = 84.62\%) confirm
that this is feasible using only bounding-box geometry. A PSR of 87.50\% means that
the system incorrectly exposes 12.5\% of bystander tracks; equivalently, 5 of the
40 bystander tracks in the evaluation dataset were mistakenly preserved unblurred.
An IPR of 84.62\% means that 15.38\% of interaction partners were unnecessarily
blurred; equivalently, 4 of 26 partner tracks were incorrectly anonymized.

Both error types carry concrete costs. A false positive (bystander preserved as
partner) constitutes a \emph{privacy leak}: the system fails to protect an
individual who did not consent to being recorded. A false negative (partner blurred
as bystander) constitutes a \emph{utility loss}: the wearer's conversation partner
is anonymized, defeating the purpose of selective anonymization. The observed ratio
of 5 FPs to 4 FNs reflects a roughly balanced trade-off, consistent with the
system's design objective of avoiding strong asymmetry in either direction.

The addition of Phase~2b landmark cues is expected to reduce false positives
specifically: the yaw veto correctly classifies faces that are turned significantly
away from the camera as bystanders, even if those faces happen to be large or
central in the frame (which would incorrectly pass the Phase~2a geometry rule). The
V-VAD boost conversely reduces false negatives by promoting faces that exhibit
sustained mouth movement to \emph{Interactor} status, even when centrality is
marginally insufficient. The net effect of Phase~2b on PSR and IPR is reported in
Table~\ref{tab:metrics_2b}.

\subsection{Comparison with BystandAR}
\label{sec:disc_bystandar}

The closest prior system, BystandAR \cite{bystandar}, achieved PSR = 98.14\% and
IPR = 96.27\% on a 16-participant evaluation with dedicated gaze-tracking and
directional microphone hardware. The proposed system achieves lower accuracy (PSR
87.50\%, IPR 84.62\%) using only a monocular RGB camera and CPU-only inference. This
performance gap is expected and attributable to two factors: the richness of input
modalities and the evaluation scale.

BystandAR uses eye gaze as a direct signal of where the wearer is looking, which is
a near-perfect proxy for interaction intent. The proposed system infers this
indirectly from face geometry and pose, which is a weaker signal subject to ambiguity
when bystanders happen to be close to the camera wearer (for example, in a crowded
corridor). Additionally, BystandAR's evaluation involved 16 controlled participant
sessions with standardised interaction protocols, whereas the present evaluation used
21 naturalistic clips without controlled conditions.

Despite this gap, the proposed system's significance lies in its deployment
constraints: it requires no gaze tracker, no directional microphone array, and no
dedicated wearable hardware. It operates on a commodity RGB camera using only
CPU-bound inference, making it directly deployable on low-cost edge devices without
hardware modification. This positions it as a practical privacy layer for consumer
AR glasses such as the Meta Ray-Ban, which lack the dedicated sensors required by
BystandAR.

\subsection{Computational Feasibility and Optimisation Pathways}
\label{sec:disc_fps}

The measured throughput of 11.62 FPS on a laptop CPU suggests that the pipeline
approaches but does not achieve 30-FPS real-time performance on desktop hardware. On
the Raspberry Pi 5, the projected throughput of approximately 3.3 FPS (Phase~2a)
falls well short of real-time requirements. Three optimisation pathways could
substantially improve this:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{ONNX export and quantisation.} Exporting YOLOv8n-face to ONNX INT8
        format and running inference via ONNXRuntime with NEON-optimised kernels for
        ARM can reduce detection latency by a factor of 2--3$\times$ on ARM hardware
        relative to the PyTorch runtime used in the current evaluation.

  \item \textbf{Reduced input resolution.} Processing frames at $416 \times 416$
        rather than $640 \times 640$ reduces YOLO inference time by approximately
        35\% with a modest reduction in detection recall for small, distant faces.
        For the target use case of close-range egocentric video, this trade-off is
        likely acceptable.

  \item \textbf{Landmark extraction batching.} In the current implementation,
        MediaPipe Face Landmarker is called once per face per frame. Batching
        multiple face crops into a single inference call would reduce per-face
        overhead, particularly when multiple faces are present simultaneously.
\end{enumerate}

With all three optimisations applied, achieving 8--12~FPS on the Raspberry Pi 5 is
plausible, which is sufficient for post-hoc processing of recorded egocentric video
(a common use case in AR privacy logging). Real-time operation at 30~FPS on the Pi~5
would likely require hardware-accelerated inference via an NPU or dedicated AI
co-processor.

\subsection{Failure Mode Analysis}
\label{sec:disc_failures}

Analysis of the five false-positive tracks (bystanders incorrectly preserved as
partners) reveals a consistent pattern: all five occurred in the \emph{interaction}
video category, where people standing near the camera wearer were never directly
addressed but occupied a large, central position in the frame for an extended
period. In these cases, the area ratio and centrality thresholds are satisfied
because the individual is physically close to the wearer, even though no interaction
occurs. This ambiguity is precisely the scenario that the Phase~2b yaw veto is
designed to address: a person who is nearby but looking in a different direction
(yaw $> 40^\circ$) should be vetoed as a bystander regardless of proximity.

The four false-negative tracks (partners incorrectly blurred) arose in two contexts:
(i) brief interactions that began after a long period in which the face was
peripheral (so the temporal latching logic had not yet committed the track to
\emph{Interactor} state), and (ii) interactions where the partner frequently turned
sideways, triggering the Phase~2b yaw veto incorrectly during the initial frames
before V-VAD had accumulated sufficient speaking evidence in its 30-frame window.
The latter failure mode represents a fundamental tension between the yaw veto
(which has high precision but can be triggered by a briefly side-on partner) and the
latching logic (which is designed to prevent false-negative flicker once a partner
is established).

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc_limitations}

The following limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of this
evaluation.

\textbf{Dataset scale and diversity.} The evaluation dataset comprises 21 clips
captured in a single indoor environment by a single camera wearer. Results may not
generalise to outdoor environments, diverse lighting conditions, non-frontal camera
heights, or culturally diverse interaction styles. A larger, multi-environment
dataset would provide a more robust assessment of generalisation.

\textbf{Single annotator ground truth.} Ground-truth labels were assigned by the
researcher alone. Although the labelling protocol is straightforward, a multi-annotator
blind annotation study with inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's~$\kappa$) would
provide stronger evidence of label reliability. The infrastructure for such a study
is fully implemented in \texttt{eval/generate\_annotation\_package.py} and
\texttt{eval/aggregate\_annotations.py}, and is identified as a near-term extension.

\textbf{Heuristic thresholds.} All eight threshold parameters (Table~\ref{tab:params})
were set empirically on the evaluation dataset itself. This risks overfitting to
the specific conditions of the recorded clips. A held-out validation set or
cross-validation procedure would provide a fairer estimate of threshold robustness.

\textbf{Absence of audio.} The V-VAD cue relies entirely on lip movement as a
speech proxy and cannot distinguish between a person mouthing words, laughing,
chewing, or speaking. Incorporating even a rudimentary microphone-based voice
activity detector as a secondary cue would substantially reduce V-VAD ambiguity at
negligible hardware cost on most wearable devices.

\textbf{Hardware projection.} The Raspberry Pi 5 throughput figure (approximately
3.3 FPS) is an estimate derived from a scaling factor rather than a direct
measurement. It should be treated as an order-of-magnitude projection; actual
performance may differ depending on OS configuration, thermal throttling, and the
version of underlying inference libraries installed.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{Summary of Contributions}
\label{sec:conclusion_summary}

This dissertation presented a context-aware, real-time privacy framework for
egocentric video that selectively anonymizes bystanders while preserving the faces
of active interaction partners. The framework operates as a five-stage sequential
pipeline: face detection via YOLOv8n-face, multi-face tracking via SORT, interaction
cue extraction, heuristic classification, and selective Gaussian blur anonymization.
All stages execute on CPU-only hardware without GPU acceleration, biometric face
recognition, or cloud offloading.

The primary contribution of this work is the \emph{multi-cue interaction
classifier}, which fuses four complementary signals derived from two information
sources. The first two cues (face area ratio and center distance) are extracted
from bounding-box geometry alone and require no additional model inference. The
second two cues (head-pose yaw via PnP and Mouth Aspect Ratio via MediaPipe Face
Landmarker) require facial landmark extraction but provide qualitatively different
information: yaw provides a hard veto for faces that are turned away from the
camera, while MAR-based V-VAD provides a temporal boost for faces exhibiting
sustained lip movement. The classifier combines these cues through a priority-ordered
decision function with temporal hysteresis and interaction latching, achieving PSR
= 87.50\% and IPR = 84.62\% in the Phase~2a geometry-only configuration, evaluated
against a ground-truth dataset of 66 unique face tracks from 21 egocentric video clips.

A secondary contribution is the \emph{systematic comparison with the blanket-blurring
baseline}. The baseline achieves PSR = 100\% but IPR = 0\%, making it unsuitable for
any use case in which the wearer needs to interact with people in the scene. The
proposed system recovers 84.62\% of interaction-partner utility (IPR) at a cost of
12.5 percentage points of PSR relative to the baseline --- a trade-off that is
appropriate for a system whose primary objective is to protect bystanders without
degrading the wearer's social experience.

A third contribution is the \emph{edge-deployable implementation}. The pipeline is
designed from the outset to run on commodity CPU hardware. The measured throughput
of 11.62~FPS on a laptop CPU, with an estimated 3.3~FPS on the Raspberry Pi~5,
establishes the feasibility of the approach on edge hardware while identifying the
optimisation opportunities needed for real-time 30-FPS deployment.

Finally, the \emph{evaluation and annotation infrastructure} developed for this
project --- including the blind annotation package generator, the inter-annotator
agreement script, and the comparative evaluation runner --- constitutes a reusable
toolkit for future egocentric privacy research.

\subsection{Relation to Prior Work}
\label{sec:conclusion_prior}

Compared to the most relevant prior system, BystandAR \cite{bystandar} (PSR~98.14\%,
IPR~96.27\%), the proposed framework achieves lower accuracy but eliminates the
hardware dependency on eye gaze trackers and directional microphones. Compared to
blanket-blurring systems such as EgoBlur \cite{egoblur2023} and SafeAR
\cite{ref:safear2025}, the proposed framework introduces the context-aware
classification layer that all prior blanket systems lack.

The proposed system is the first, to the author's knowledge, to combine:
(i) real-time selective anonymization in egocentric video,
(ii) CPU-only edge deployment without biometric recognition,
(iii) a multi-cue fusion of geometry, head pose, and V-VAD, and
(iv) a quantitative evaluation distinguishing PSR from IPR as separate privacy and
utility metrics.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion_future}

Several directions are identified for future investigation.

\textbf{Learned classifier.} The heuristic decision function could be replaced with
a lightweight learned binary classifier (e.g., a shallow MLP or logistic regression
model trained on the cue vectors) that generalises better across environments and
interaction styles than hand-tuned thresholds.

\textbf{Audio-visual fusion.} Incorporating a microphone-based voice activity
detector alongside the visual V-VAD cue would resolve ambiguous lip-movement cases
and substantially improve IPR in noisy environments.

\textbf{Multi-annotator blind evaluation.} The annotation infrastructure implemented
in this project enables a rigorous multi-annotator study with inter-rater agreement
measurement (Cohen's~$\kappa$). Applying this methodology to a larger, more
diverse dataset would provide stronger evidence of generalisation.

\textbf{On-device deployment.} Direct benchmarking on the Raspberry Pi~5 with ONNX
INT8 quantised models, combined with the input-resolution and batching optimisations
described in Section~5.3, would validate or refine the throughput projection and
identify any architecture-specific performance bottlenecks not captured by the
current scaling-factor estimate.

\textbf{Consent and transparency mechanisms.} The current system operates
invisibly from the bystander's perspective. Future work could integrate BystandARIA's
visible-light communication approach \cite{ref:bystandaria} as a complementary
consent channel, allowing bystanders to actively signal their privacy preferences
to the system.
